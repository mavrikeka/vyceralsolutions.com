Are your decisions "Data Informed"?

Created on 2016-11-28 02:44

Published on 2016-11-28 04:59

I just finished Charles Duhigg's latest book - Smarter, Faster, Better and by the end of it, I was asking myself the question - Are the decisions I made 'Data Informed'? Some background on myself - I am an engineer, have an MBA, can write some SQL, work for a company that has the stock symbol 'DATA' and in a personality assessment test (DISC) my personality type was 'Analytical'. So you would think that the answer would be obvious.

I was in for a rude surprise when I started reading some examples in the book. For example, there was Project Oxygen. A data informed project by the people analytics team at Google that set out to answer the question - 'Do managers matter' and then leveraged an analytical approach to actually identify what makes a good manager.

There was the example of how the Cincinnati Public Schools turned around some of their worst performing schools by teaching principals and teachers how to collect and use data to improve the performance of every individual student.

There were quite a few more such examples and as I read the book it became obvious that there are 3 steps to making 'Data Informed' decisions -

As I looked back at decisions I made and the effort I put in making those decisions, I realized that some of them fell into the 'Just Do It' bucket - Like hitting tennis balls against a wall or hitting golf balls at the driving range. The idea here was that if I hit enough balls I would get better. Or that time when I would cold call a bunch of people and hope that just the sheer volume of calls would get me a response.

The next phase of evolution for me was 'Have A Plan' and till I read the book I thought this is what being 'data informed' meant. I'd look at some data and based on the data I'd come up with a plan and then I'd execute on the plan. For example - A lot of sales people are successful if their customers have a 'shared service' and a 'community of practice'. So I would focus on making sure that my customers had the same. I would proudly inform my manager that this was my plan and then execute against the plan. This method worked till it stopped working and when it stopped working, I had no idea what to do. I needed a new plan but I didn't know where to start. Oftentimes, I would not know that the plan was not working till the end of the year when a couple big deals didn't happen and I missed my number.

What I realized about being truly 'data informed' was to realize that I needed data to come up with a plan, but one didn't stop there. I then had to create hypotheses and test those hypotheses. For example, rather than saying cold calling is dead because I called a bunch of people and no one responded (technically the data showed that it was not working) what if I applied a 'data informed' approach. Cold calling depends on various factors. Let's say they are - the messenger (me), the message, the receiver (customer), the time of call and the title of the receiver. What if I ran a bunch of tests, changed one variable at a time (so I really understood the causality) and imagine if I found out - Cold calling a CXO does not work irrespective of the message, but changing the messenger (using my manager) works. Cold calling a Director at 9 am on a Fri has a better response rate than other days of the week at 9 am etc. What I end up with are scenarios that work and those that don't. The beauty is that these aren't theories but based on real customer feedback. I end up applying a scientific process with A/B testing.

In the book, there is an example of a manager at JP Morgan Chase collections department that followed this process and ended up with insights like - "it’s better to call people’s homes between 9:15 And 11:50 AM, because, the wife will pick up and women are more likely to pay off a family’s debts.” Similar examples are shared around the Cincinnati Public Schools. Regular people apply data to their jobs and getting extraordinary results.

Obviously the big objection is that this is a lot of work. Capturing all that data, running all those tests but then I ask myself - is there a better way? 'Just Do It' makes you feel good because you are busy but deep down you know you are working hard not smart. 'Having a Plan' works when there is no change and you can run the same play again and again, but as we know if there is one thing that is a constant, it is change and by the time you realize something has changed, you have been disrupted.

The other objection is that this is not natural for most of us. I gave one simplistic example of how I could use a 'data informed' approach to make my job as a salesperson better. I am sure there are many others that I am not aware of. Organizations realize that being 'data informed' makes them more competitive and innovative and I think that organizations will be investing in teaching their employees how to 'Think with Data' and not just use the dashboards / tools that are available.

I guess to wrap up - I need to start with a hypothesis if this will actually work and then test it out.

btw.. there is a whole subculture about 'Data Driven' vs 'Data Informed' that I didn't know existed, till I started writing this post.
