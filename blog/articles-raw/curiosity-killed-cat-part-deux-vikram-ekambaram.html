Curiosity killed the cat - Part deux

Created on 2016-02-22 12:18

Published on 2016-02-26 23:09

Some time ago I wrote a post titled - "Did curiosity kill the cat". I had been curious how that saying came about and I could not find anything conclusive on the origin of the saying. Part of the reason I was curious about curiosity killing the cat was because it was not in line with my perception about curiosity - that curiosity leads to innovation and something you want to develop (unlike what
curiosity killed the cat tends to imply).

Fast forward a few months and I am reading a book "Curious" by Ian Leslie and the author talks about 3 types of curiosity - diversive, epistemic and empathic. Diversive curiosity is like an itch that needs to be scratched. A child sees a flame
and wonders what will happen if he puts his finger in the flame or you see a shiny object and you get distracted and want to explore.  In fact, high diversive curiosity is a risk factor for drug addiction and arson. In this context, I totally get how curiosity can kill the cat or at least hurt the cat.

Epistemic curiosity on the other hand is the kind of curiosity that leads to deeper research to build understanding. For example, in my case I was curious about curiosity killed the cat. I did some research on the web and could not find anything conclusive, so then I started reading up about curiosity, wrote about it, got some responses and then I stumbled upon a book about being curious. This kind of curiosity that leads to a deeper exploration about a topic is definitely worth cultivating and helped me answer my own question.

Ian Leslie in his book has a very interesting perspective about how tools like Google instead of making us more curious make us less curious. This seems counter-intuitive because you would think that with access to all that information, people would be more curious. Ian's point is that since Google does such a good job of providing people answers, they don't have to do the hard research that let's you really get deep into a topic. He compares it to solving a puzzle vs unraveling a mystery. You learn a lot more by unraveling a mystery. Also, just knowing that you can have the answer at your fingertips makes you lazy. The need to accumulate information goes down because you can get it whenever you want.

For example, if I had found a search result that explained diversive curiosity and how it related to my question on the origin of curiosity killed the cat, I would never have written about it (since the answer was out there, I would not have reached out to all of you to get your perspective) and I would certainly not have read a book that provided me a new perspective on curiosity and other topics. What I've realized is that exploring and finding an answer (unraveling the mystery) is satisfying at a deeper level than being spoon-fed the answer (solving a puzzle). Solving a puzzle gives you a short-term high while unraveling a mystery provides long term satisfaction.
