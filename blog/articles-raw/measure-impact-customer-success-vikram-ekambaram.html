I recently got a recommendation from a friend (thanks @Chris Li) to read a book A More Beautiful Question by Warren Berger. I strongly feel that curiosity is a key skill we all need to develop as we try to keep abreast of the rapid change and disruption we face in our work environments. I've written a couple articles on the topic of curiosity (here, here and here) and this book of questions just hit the spot (especially as I've been writing about disruption lately).

One of the techniques they talk about is QFT (Question Formulation Technique) by the Right Question Institute. I won't go into detail about QFT, you can watch some of the videos and read about it but broadly they talk about is starting with a question focus (QFocus) and then come up with a list of questions, identify close and open ended questions, convert statements to questions, convert a few open ended questions into close ended question and vice versa. Finally prioritize and come up with an action plan (I am missing a couple steps, but you get the idea).

Being the 'learn by doing' type I decided to put it into action (I will caveat this by saying that the intent is for it to be a group exercise to get different perspectives and to get people to come up with their own answers - so I am going to see if I can get a group response to this blog and try to do this exercise virtually).

QFocus - Measure the impact of a Customer Success program

Some context - Like many other software companies we have made a big investment in Customer Success and the question I ask myself is - I manage a team that costs Tableau let's say $5M (I just picked a round number). If this were my money and it was my company, how would I justify this investment? If someone came and said, I think you should spend another $5M and double the program how would I decide if I should or not?

So the next step is to come up with a list of questions (and just to be clear, the next step would be to have folks add their own questions. Once that is done, we can prioritize them and see what plan we need to come up with).

My Questions

  • What % return on the $5M will Tableau consider a good investment?
  • How can we isolate impact to just the Customer Success program investment?
  • What other factors contribute to a customer's success?
  • How do we define 'Success' for a customer?
  • How do I run an A/B test at scale (statistically significant sample) that isolates impact of just the Customer Success program investment?
  • What data do I have as a baseline to start from?
  • What is the time frame to measure the impact of the Customer Success program?
  • Can we truly measure the impact of a Customer Success program or are we just providing some numbers to justify an investment?
  • If 'Customer Success' is a team sport, should we bother isolating impact of a Customer Success program?
  • How do companies justify investments in other teams / programs?
  • Why don't Netflix, Uber, Facebook have a Customer Success program?
  • Do Enterprise software vendors need a Customer Success program because their products are too complicated/pricey as compared to Consumer products?
  • Do Enterprises buy software for people who don't want to use the software and so the need for a Customer Success program?
  • If Enterprise Software was as dialed in to the need of consumers as Netflix / Uber, would we still need a Customer Success program?

I could go on much longer but feel this is a good stopping point. Curious what questions others have / can add to this and curious to see if this will work virtually. One last point is that the intent of this post is not to find a new metric to measure Customer Success. I think Net Retention Rate, Lifetime Customer Value are great measures to know if a customer is successful. The question is, how much did my investment in a Customer Success program affect that and how do I know that it wasn't due to that investment.
