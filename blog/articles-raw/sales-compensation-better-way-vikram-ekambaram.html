Sales Compensation - A better way

Created on 2015-11-15 16:06

Published on 2015-11-15 16:53

This is the 3rd post in what initially started out as a standalone post around my frustrations with sales compensation that tends to be highly demotivating (unless you are successful). Then I looked at disruptions in sales process and sales culture over the last few years. While people agree with what I've written, the question that keeps coming back is - What is the better way? Do I have any ideas. This question has been tumbling in my head over the last couple weeks and here is my idea. Obviously this is one option and I am sure that others have better ideas. Not sure if this is even feasible, but it helps to iterate.

I'll start with how I blew out my number last year. I was new to Tableau and I was given 15 accounts and a $3.7M quota. It was January and I knew this was a pretty high quota. I also knew that two of my largest accounts had just done a big deal in Q4 (so they would be consuming licenses). This was confirmed when I met with our key sponsors at those account and they said that they had just done a 'big deal' and would not be looking to doing another big deal this year. Having come from prior jobs where the new guy got screwed, I decided that this would be my 'investment' year. The one thing that was different, from prior jobs, was that I could sell my product to other lines of business (so no account was ever 'sold out'). I could also start small with a business group or analyst and then grow over time. Again, unlike other companies, management fully supported this 'land and expand' strategy and there was lots of proof that reps had sold millions with this strategy.

So, I talked to a lot of the successful reps and came up with a strategy that focused on key outcomes. The assumption was that I controlled these key outcomes and if I delivered on these key outcomes the revenue would follow. The beauty of this strategy was that I took the one thing I did not control (how will I sell 3.7M) out of the mix. If I had focused on that, I would have been stressed and gotten negative. So what were the key outcomes I focused on -

1. Establishing us as a BI Standard

2. Setting up a Tableau Center of Excellence

3. Establishing a Shared Service supporting Tableau

4. Regular lunch and learns

5. Doing an annual Tableau Innovation Day

6. Were we invited to their Tech Expo

7. Did we have a pricing agreement and contract in place

8. Was there a Tableau Steering committee. Was I part of it?

9. Did they send people to the Tableau Conference

10. Had I documented their use cases?

11. Were they using Tableau PS or partners to drive adoption.

12. Executive awareness.

I had talked to our most successful reps and looked at our top accounts and they all had these as common attributes. To fast forward - halfway through the year I was at 30% of my number and I had no big deals in my pipeline. Luckily, I had 'landed' a lot of new groups and I was making them successful. The flood gates opened in the second half and I ended up blowing out my number, but if I hadn't kept my head down and focused on delivering to the outcomes above, I would have taken my eye off the ball and failed.

So what does all this have to do with coming up with a new way to compensate salespeople. Imagine if I was compensated on driving this behavior in addition to making my number? This way if something unforeseen came up and I did not make my number but I still got 'recognition', at least I would not be de-motivated. You can also now manage out reps, who don't align with your beliefs.

This also ties in with my earlier post about what is a manager supposed to do. The manager's role in this scenario, is to work with the salesperson to come up with the list of behaviors. Suddenly, sales moves from this nebulous state where we don't really understand why someone is successful or why someone isn't, to coming up with key behaviors to deliver on the outcomes. These behaviors should not be fixed but should be iterated on (every quarter). So you can tweak things that don't work. To assume that you can come up with a plan that does not change is folly. Also, this allows reps to provide feedback on what is working and what is not working. So suddenly you have the buy in from your rep. The role of the manager is to be aware of systemic issues that will prevent reps from delivering on the behaviors. The quarterly tweaking of expectations, also creates a constant feedback loop around performance management.

There should also be a way to celebrate and recognize excellence. This excellence should be defined not just based on deals being done (they already get commission for that), but based on delivering on the behaviors. Again, the role of the manager moves from managing just pipeline and quota to managing, collaborating and motivating reps.

The one downside of this is that you might not be able to hire those reps that are money motivated. The ones that want to blow out the numbers and make a lot of money. As this comp plan will smooth out the commissions and there won't be fewer highs or lows. The question to ask is - would you rather have 90% of your employees motivated and making good money or would you rather have 10% blow it out and 40% not motivated. Will you make more money by have 90% making their number while losing a few of your top performers? I don't know the answer to this. Also, is it better to hire mercenaries or people that align with your beliefs.

Does this work in other areas - I read about how Tony Dungy turned around the Bucs. He did not focus on wins. Instead he focused on making sure that everyone had the right 'habits'. Once the habits were internalized the wins followed. Why not do the same with sales. Lastly, I know organizations that have a combination of MBOs and Commission, but what I've noticed is that the MBOs aren't rigorously defined and recognition is always based on revenue not behavior.

Just another curiosity driven post on a Sunday. While I don't impact any decisions or drive any decisions, it is good to keep noodling.
