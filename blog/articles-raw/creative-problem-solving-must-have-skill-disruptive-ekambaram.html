Creative Problem Solving - Must have skill in a disruptive world

Created on 2017-02-15 00:49

Published on 2017-02-15 02:11

My son takes part in an after school program called Destination Imagination. DI as it is known, is all about creative problem solving. A couple weeks ago I got to appraise an Instant Challenge (IC) fiesta. An Instant Challenge is basically a 5-10 min challenge that a team (around 5-7 kids) is given. It can be a 'building' challenge or an 'acting' challenge or a hybrid. In a building challenge, the kids are given a set of materials that they have to use to solve the challenge. This is a link to what a building instant challenge would look like.

It is a great representation of challenges we face at work - time constraints, resource constraints and collaboration issues. You get points based on solving the challenge, teamwork and creativity of the solution. It is also a great representation of challenges we face in a disruptive world, in that, we need to solve a problem that we have never solved before. There used to be a time that the pace of change was slow and we could make 5 year plans and what worked in the past would work in the future. So the skills needed to succeed were operational - find more efficiency, deliver, project manage etc.

Considering the rapid pace of disruption looking to the past is not going to be a good indicator of the future. Uber and AirBnB did not exist 10 years ago. So chances are to survive in a disruptive world, you have to solve problems that there is no past formula you can follow.

The challenge the teams had to solve at the fiesta was to build a bridge that could hold the most weight. They were given materials like straws, pencils, mailing labels etc. As I watched different teams compete a few things stood out for the teams that did well, that I felt were great lessons for me to apply at work -

  1. They quickly figured out what problem they needed to solve - This is my pet peeve on some work projects. I am very quick to jump to solutioning, despite not having clearly understood the problem that needs to be solved. I assume I know what the problem is and I often come up with solutions to the wrong problem. One of the teams built the best looking and most intricate bridge but it did not hold any weight. They assumed they had to build a bridge but forgot about the weight bearing aspect. So now I ask myself - what is the problem I need to solve and how will I know if I am successful?
  2. They brainstormed out of the box ideas but did not take up the entire time brainstorming. At some point they went with good enough - Brainstorming is important as it lets teams come up with many ideas but often there are projects where this goes on forever as the teams try to come up with the perfect idea. Sometimes I have to remind myself to to stop at good enough. There is no perfect idea. So applying artificial deadlines helps move the process along.
  3. The brainstorming involved demonstrating the idea with a minimum viable product - The minimum viable product in the brainstorming phase was critical as it helped others understand the idea by touching / seeing it (not just talking about it). Rather than saying I'd like to use the pencils as the base of the bridge, they would actually show this in action. I am an idea person and it is easy to come up with grand ideas but lately I am realizing that whenever I have something to show / demo, people grasp the idea much better. Seeing the idea come to life, creates an emotional connection for people (good or bad).
  4. Once brainstorming was done, it was all about focusing efforts on tweaking the structure to make it better - Since there was not enough time to go back to the drawing board, the teams would rapidly iterate in making what they had better. When they placed a weight and the structure did not hold the weight, they narrowed the problem to how it could hold the weight. If the structure had to be longer they found a way to use the materials to make it longer. In work projects, I have the tendency to get down on myself when something does not work or I get negative feedback. It is easy to want to dump everything and either give up or start afresh. I've noticed that having a minimum viable product has helped me get very specific feedback. This has been invaluable as people can tell me the one thing that is not working and I can now isolate and fix it.
  5. The last key point, was to always have a working version of the product - There wasn't time to be discussing theory. If you had an idea on making the product better it wasn't about who had the stronger opinion. You did it and either it worked or it didn't and either way you learned something that helped with the next iteration. I've been in many meetings where hours are spent arguing about opinions and all that happens is that people get really attached to their opinions and eventually the highest paid person decides on the path forward (HiPPO). This has been the biggest learning for me personally. I have to catch myself in meetings where I tend to spout opinions as fact and instead to remind myself that all I have is a hypothesis (and I might believe that mine is the best hypothesis but it still is a hypothesis). This then opens up the conversation to figure out what experiment to run to validate the hypothesis.

So what stopped teams from actually trying new things -

  • Not understanding the real problem
  • Fear of failure
  • Not having confidence in their idea
  • Inertia to trying something new
  • Team ridicule.

The key for me is to give myself permission to fail, but also knowing that there is no success or failure, it is just learning - "The road to success is paved by the stones of failure".
