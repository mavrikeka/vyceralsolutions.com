Enabling lasting change

Created on 2015-11-19 12:02

Published on 2015-11-19 13:51

In an earlier post I had talked about needing to be a 'solution enabler' to drive adoption across groups. I was very aware that just creating something that I thought was awesome and then assuming that people would adopt, would not work. What I was not clear about was - what strategy would work. I've been doing a fair bit of reading lately and spent a lot of time experimenting with design thinking concepts. Start with the Why, so I can get alignment on belief. Daniel Pink on motivation. They all made sense but something was missing. In the back of my mind, I've always had a nagging question - how do I make sure that the adoption of the ideas lasts? 

The chart above is the average number of steps I take as captured by my smartphone. I got a new smartphone in March and it automatically captures my steps. The sad part is that, the chart above is true about a lot of things I do. Starts off high, then over time starts petering off. The outlier in July (11740) was because I spent a month in London and did a lot of walking (it was a fun way to explore the city). Once I came back and got into my normal routine, the downward trend started.

It is obvious that I am not a big fan of exercise. While there are certain interventions that drive 'temporary' change (getting a new tracking device, new city etc.) it does not last. I have many such graphs in my life. I went to 'sales training', came back excited, was gung-ho for the first few months and then it petered off. Maybe even my recent spurt of blogging. I'm excited, I'm blogging a lot but will it too taper off?

Which brings me to the topic of this post - How do you enable lasting change? Is it true that people don't change? That 'crisis', 'interventions', 'training' etc. cause a short term bump that then peters back to average?

I decided to stop focusing on my 'failures' and look at instances where I have had lasting change. The one example that comes to mind is tennis. I started playing tennis in 2008, I was excited, it was a new sport I was learning. I joined a USTA league found a group to play with at my gym and started competing at my level. The amateur competition really got be motivated. I worked my way up the ratings. I got hurt a lot initially, so I started exercising / stretching. I exercised for a couple years to get in shape. I went gluten-free (and still am for the most part). I was uber-competitive and was on teams that competed regionally and nationally. Then in 2013 I became a 4.0 (decent club player rating) and I got a new job. This is usually the time in my life where I switch from one passion to another (I did this with golf in 2008). My tennis was plateauing, I wasn't winning as much at the higher level and my job was more fun. I started playing less tennis, I stopped exercising as much. I wasn't as driven to win in my inter-club  tennis matches.

Was the dreaded downward trend going to happen and was this bye, bye tennis. It's now been two years and I am still playing tennis and passionate about it. So what was different this time? I realized that it came down to 'community'. A lot of my friends are tennis friends, my wife and I play mixed together, we take a tennis vacation with close friends,my kids now play tennis. I still love competing and now I have become a good 4.0, so I am winning more than losing. Tennis has become a part of my life.

Which makes me realize that if we want to drive lasting change, we have to work on creating a community of like minded people that will be part of our journey as we learn the new habits. This community will also keep us from giving up when it is tough to keep going (and often it is tough and the reward is not obvious and the only thing that keeps us going is our community lending a supportive ear). This community will teach us when we are stuck and need help. We will also contribute back to the community, thus giving us a sense of purpose and meaning.

I though the answer was to figure out how to get people to 'believe', because if they 'believed' then they would adopt. So start with the 'Why' and find people with beliefs that align, but in reality beliefs don't always align and sometimes you need to go on blind faith. I remember when I joined Tableau, I did not have much 'belief' that management always knew what the right strategy was. Would this 'land and expand' thing help me be successful? I had had been burnt in the past. What I did do was suspend 'disbelief' and went on faith. Luckily I had my support system (my peers) to keep me on track. We had weekly calls as a team where I could voice my doubts, I talked to my inside sales rep about my doubts and he would often talk me off the ledge. All I needed was the support of a community telling me that I was doing the right things, even if at that time it didn't feel like it. At the end of the year, it all worked out but the initial journey was tough and if my peer community hadn't been there to support me, I would not have stuck with the plan.

At Tableau, we've seen this at our customers. Where we have user groups that form and they drive adoption of the product.We have key champions that are passionate and when a few of them get together a community forms and the adoption grows.

There is a great book I am reading called the Power of Habit, by Charles Duhigg that in my mind really nails this topic. Curious what others have experienced when it comes to enabling lasting change. Is creating a community the secret to lasting change?
