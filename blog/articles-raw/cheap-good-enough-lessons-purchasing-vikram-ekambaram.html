Cheap and good enough - Lessons in purchasing

Created on 2016-10-21 18:13

Published on 2016-10-24 13:12

If I have the option to buy something that is cheap and good enough, chances are I will buy that product. That's just me. I like to think that I am being smart with my spending choices, my family thinks I am cheap. I think I am finding the best deal, they think I am penny wise, pound foolish.

It reminds me of a time last year when I was looking to buy a phone for my 13 year old. He really really wanted a phone. As someone that believes in making decisions based on logic and rationality as opposed to emotion, I spent some time with him to jot down the reasons he wanted a phone.

The reasons were -

  1. Everyone else in my class has one.
  2. I need to text with my friends and be able to call and talk to them.

I then applied my "cheap and good enough" filter to his requirements and ended up getting him a flip phone. I wish I could say that the story ended there. Sadly, it didn't. Apart from the fact that it wasn't cool, his biggest complaint was that he hated texting on it. It had one of those keyboards where you have to press the keys a couple times before you got the letter you wanted. If you want the letter 'C' you press the number 2 key three times.

From a purely logical and money standpoint I could not understand why that was such a big issue. Texts are not very verbose. To me it seemed like a minor inconvenience and not a reason to have to buy him an expensive smartphone with an expensive data plan.

So my son asked me to use the phone to text and I realized how much I hated having to press 3 keys to get the letter I wanted. I was so used to my smartphone keyboard that I couldn't give up that simplicity and intuitiveness. Needless to say he ended up getting a smartphone.

The moral of the story for me was -

  1. It is easy to make a decision based on cheap and good enough when you are not the end user. This is the trap that I feel IT / Procurement tend to do when buying software for the business.
  2. You can make the best choice based on a written set of requirements but until your end users actually use what you buy, what seems like logical trade offs can become barriers to adoption. I had posted about this some time back when I looked at the RFP/POC process.
  3. Once your end users have experienced 'better' it is tough to expect them to go back to 'good enough'. Many companies/products have become irrelevant because of this (e.g Blackberry) and companies end up with a lot of shelf-ware because they got 'good enough' products bundled in their Enterprise Agreements but the users don't leverage the 'free' products.
  4. Lastly, don't underestimate the importance of 'cool' and 'peer pressure' when it comes to a product being used by the end user. When your peers are doing something cool and looking good, you want to be part of it. The beauty here is the adoption is driven bottoms up and not by some IT/Leadership mandate that requires everyone to use the new product.
