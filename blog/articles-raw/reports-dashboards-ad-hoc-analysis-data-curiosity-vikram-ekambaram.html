Reports, Dashboards and Ad-Hoc Analysis - Data Curiosity in Organizations

Created on 2016-03-07 01:25

Published on 2016-03-07 02:43

I've been curious about curiosity. I've been blogging about it - first this one and then this one. I believe that organizations that have curious employees will be more innovative and be able to survive better in this disruptive world we live in.

This topic is about "Data Curiosity". How curious is every employee in an organization about the data they deal with? With the amount of data growing exponentially, this skill becomes critical.

In general, when it comes to data, I've found that business people (not experts / data scientists) fall somewhere in the spectrum below.

They are often afraid of the data because it seems too complicated or they are indifferent to it because analyzing data is boring. Then there are those business folks that thrive with data and actively look for data to analyze and support their decisions.

I've noticed that there are 2 things that define where people fall in this spectrum

- How is the data / analysis presented to them - Reports / Dashboards / Raw Data?

- What tools are available to business users to analyze the data?

Reports

I am reminded of a time about 10 years ago when I used to get credit card statements in the mail. Every month a thick paper statement came to my house and it was a list of all my transactions. I hated looking at my credit card statement. There was a ton of data in it - what I spent money on, when I spent money, how much I spent money on etc. but all I did every month was go through every transaction and make sure that it was something I had spent money on. Was I curious about the data, absolutely not. I looked to answer 1 question - Did I recognize the transaction? This took me 15 mins once every month and then I promptly shredded the statement. I still do this today, its just that the statement is online. If you want to kill data curiosity, the best way is to send a report with a bunch of rows and columns. There are some professions - auditors / finance folks etc. who need to go through every single data element in detail but they are the exception.

Dashboards

A few years ago my credit card company started providing me some dashboards. I could look at spend by category, merchant and I could filter to a time period. The dashboard answered quite a few more questions for me and now I spend 30 mins every month looking at the categories I spend money on.

Dashboards have increased my data curiosity. They engage me a little more with what in the past was just a bunch of transactions. Now I am curious on where I spend my money, who do I spend the most money with etc. On the spectrum of data curiosity a good dashboard definitely moves one to the middle. What's missing in a dashboard is that it only answers a predefined set of questions that Amex has pre-built and it does not aggregate data from my other credit cards. Definitely better than the list of transactions but it doesn't answer the questions that invariably come up when one's curiosity is aroused - On average how much do I spend eating out? How does this compare with my spend from last year? Are there any months I eat out more? Are there specific restaurants I eat at? How much have I spent with USTA over the years? How does this spend look across all my credit cards and debit card etc.?

Ad-Hoc Analysis

Once people get engaged with their data, they becomes curious. They have more questions and the more answers they get, the more questions they have. To answer all these new questions, involves me downloading the raw data from Amex and playing around with it. The de-facto standard for this ad-hoc analysis is Excel and Excel is not fun. To answer any of the questions above, I have to struggle with pivot tables / pivot charts etc. It is not intuitive and this is the biggest curiosity killer in organizations. When the tool is not intuitive and fun, you don't want to do any analysis. So the ad-hoc analysis (like the dashboard) gets relegated to answering the same basic questions every month and one never gets into the deeper analysis. When I look at the curiosity spectrum, an activity that I could really engage in - asking questions of my data - instead ends up with me being indifferent to the data - because of the tool available to me.

In summary, the lesson for organizations that are interested in a fostering a data driven culture. A culture where the people are curious about their data. A culture where business users can ask any question of their data - "you can't just stop at providing dashboards that answer a predefined set of questions". You need to encourage your business users to do ad-hoc analysis and you need to make the data accessible to them (in a secure way - I don't want other getting access to my credit card data) and provide them a tool that is intuitive and fun to use. There are lots of tools that claim to be easy and intuitive to use - but there is only one way to know - actually use the tool.

As Ian Leslie describes in his book Curious about puzzles vs mysteries - A dashboard is like solving a puzzle while ad-hoc analysis is like investigating a mystery. Unlike what most people think - A good dashboard is usually the starting point for a journey of ad-hoc analysis, it is not the destination. So like Sherlock Holmes with his magnifying glass, pick up the right tool to get going on the journey of ad-hoc analysis.
