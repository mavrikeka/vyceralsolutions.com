Problem Solver vs Solution Enabler

Created on 2015-11-03 23:53

Published on 2015-11-04 00:43

As an individual contributor I have been a very good problem solver. My solutions have been - pick your buzz word - "out of the box", "creative", "innovative" etc. For most of my life I defined myself and my success by how good a problem solver I was. It didn't matter if this was work related or something else. When I started getting injured playing tennis, I spent a lot of time reading up on chronic vs acute injuries, trigger points, inflammation, cold vs. heat therapy, kinesio taping. When I played a tennis match, I would deconstruct my opponents strengths and weaknesses to come up with a winning strategy. If I had a customer that didn't get the value proposition of my product, I spent time understanding their need, what value we added and went back with a solution. If there was a problem I wanted to find the solution.

As you can imagine this makes me quite popular with my customers, managers etc. (not necessarily my wife - sometimes you just need to listen and there isn't a problem to be solved).

Lately, I have been involved in finding solutions to problems that require people to adopt the solution. When it was just me, it was easy. When it comes to a  group of people, it doesn't work that well anymore. I could come up with the best idea or solution and everyone would agree that it was the right thing to do, but 'change' did not happen. It has made me question my approach and I have realized that while being a 'problem solver' is good, to drive change across groups you need to be a 'solution enabler'.

'Solution enablement' goes beyond 'solution finding' whereby you are not just finding solutions that are relevant, you are actually taking yourself out of the equation. It is no longer your solution.

Being a 'solution enabler' means that you don't have to be the one with all the ideas. You want to facilitate others coming up with the solution. By letting go of being the 'smartest' person, I can get more people involved. What  this then leads to is that the people affected by the change feel ownership of the solution. Which means they will probably adopt it as it is their idea. What was even more insightful was that often the solutions a group came up with was way better than anything I could think of. My solutions were good but theirs was exceptional. Sometimes I would come up with the first iteration and then it would take a life of its own.

I am realizing that this new skill is probably a lot more valuable than being the problem solver. To add to my earlier post about 'People power - what should a manager do' - I think being a 'solution enabler' is a key skill for managers. People are awesome and given the freedom will come up with real solutions that they will adopt. It also is less pressure on managers as they no longer have to be the ones to come up with the ideas and then find ways to motivate their people to adopt those ideas (back to the carrot and stick approach).
