Machine Learning and Organizational Design

Created on 2020-07-19 13:22

Published on 2020-07-19 14:20

Before there was Machine Learning, there were expert systems. Without getting too much into the details of expert systems (I am no expert on this topic), the basic idea was that the designer knew the rules beforehand and built the rules into the system. This worked great when the rules were known and the system did not change a lot. So expert systems were great for a world that was complicated. Where expert systems struggled was when the world became complex. In a world of constant disruption, having a system that was pre-coded with the rules was too rigid. Any changes, required the designer to change the system and this was a big effort. By the time the changes were made and deployed, the world had changed again.

Machine Learning came about to solve this complexity. Instead of hardcoded rules, the system would learn the rules thus making it much more resilient and adaptable, when there were a lot of rules to be learned. You still need a designer to design the system and put the governance in place, but instead of having to add new rules when the world changed, the system would realize something had changed and adapt. Some concepts from Silicon Valley that apply are - "Learn by Doing", "Fail fast", "There is no such thing as failure, just learning".

This brings me to the topic of the post - Organizational Design and Machine Learning. In his book Team of Teams, General McChrystal talks about how the Special Ops Task Force had to completely rethink their organizational design to better adapt to a complex world. They had to shift from an 'expert system' paradigm to a 'machine learning' paradigm. The reason was because the enemy was adapting rapidly and there was no way for the traditional 'Command and Control' approach to keep up. Once a decision was made and the new 'rule' was encoded and new procedures were rolled out, the enemy would be stymied for a short period of time (a few days) and then adapt to the new 'rule'. This then required more new 'rules' to be developed (takes months) and it became obvious that the 'expert system' approach was flawed in this complex world.


This is the challenge a lot of organizations face. When faced with complexity / disruption, they tend to go up the chain to 'come up with the new plan'. The leaders get together with their strategy teams, get input from the field and then after a few months, come out with a new 'strategy'. The field teams are then given a strategy to execute and often it does not work perfectly and even if it does, the world soon changes and the strategy is obsolete. So then one goes back up the chain to come up with the new strategy for the next disruption.

General McChrystal talks about the need to push the planning and execution down the ranks. He sees the role of leaders to set the vision and create a 'shared consciousness' and the role of the people in the field to be 'empowered to execute'. What comes with this, is the understanding that people will make mistakes and the intent is to learn and get better not chastise and punish. This is exactly how Machine Learning works. The designers create the models and then the Machine Learning algorithms go to work. Initially, the ML will lose a lot. This is because they learn by making mistakes but as they make mistakes they learn and get better and they adapt. If anyone is interested in a low tech understanding of how this works, here is a great article - Matchbox AI.

In his blog around 'Self-Managing organizations', Lex Sisney talks about the role of leadership in a self managed organization and the parallels are very similar to the role of the designer in Machine Learning. He actually talks about a little tweak to 'Team of Teams' and 'Reinventing Organizations' (both great reads, if you are looking to understanding more about Self Managing Organizations) where he sees the role of leadership as 'Architecting the game for others to play'. General McChrystal calls the role of the leader as a 'Gardener'.

This role of leaders as designers is key. This provides the framework for success. Without this there will be chaos as there is no structure to execute and everyone can optimize for whatever they want. One distinction I'd like to make is that the leaders work with the team (or input from a subset of the team) to create a 'shared consciousness' / 'design the game'. The above diagram, makes it seem like there is a separation in who designs and who plays the game. I think, the players need to be part of the design.

We see this with Facebook, YouTube, Twitter. While they started out with 'empowered execution'. As an individual, you have the power to create the content and own the narrative. This is great, but there are downsides, without any governance, to this 'empowered execution' - as we are seeing with the social media platforms. A few bad actors or even good actors with the best intentions, can still lead to some really bad unintended consequences. So you need leadership to create a framework within which to operate. Whether we call this governance or structure or shared consciousness, this is key for this approach to work.

Bottomline, I think that just like Machine Learning is here to stay, organizations will need to start migrating towards a more 'design centric' approach to leadership. The rate of disruption and complexity is not going to slow down and approaches that are rigid and don't adapt are only going to sow the seeds of further disruption and leave employees demotivated and unengaged.
