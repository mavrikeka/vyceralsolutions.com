Tableau Dashboards and Design Thinking

Created on 2016-05-22 13:02

Published on 2016-05-22 14:13

I am not formally trained in Design Thinking but have been curious about it, dabbled in it and talked the ear off anyone who will listen to me about it. In fact this week my son will participate in a global competition in Tennessee as part of Destination Imagination. A competition which teaches kids creative problem solving. To me Design Thinking incorporates various concepts that I've blogged about - Fail fast, learn by doing, agile, curiosity, problem solving etc.

I've talked to a lot of folks about how Tableau dashboard building is a great practical application of Design Thinking but lacking the formal training, I could not articulate it well enough in the language of Design Thinking. I woke up today and saw a post from Bridget Cogley around this topic that inspired me to write this. Anyone who wants to know more about Tableau dashboard building and Design Thinking should read that post.

As dashboard builders we are often asked to do some analysis / build a dashboard and we are asked to start at 'Step 3 - What Wows'. I'm often told - "We need to build a dashboard that is visually stunning". Then you are handed some data / requirements / questions. Another variation is - "Here is a report we have built in another tool and we want you to recreate it and make it pretty". Or, there is the - "I've built this dashboard in Tableau that answers these questions / provides these KPIs, can you polish it".

The tendency is then to jump into 'Step 2 - What if' and create a better version of the dashboard and then send it to the 'consumer' of the dashboard, whos reaction is - "This is not exactly what I wanted". So then we loop around in Step 2 and keep iterating (independent of the 'consumer') till either the 'consumer' gets frustrated at the time this takes and your inability to read their mind or you get frustrated because it seems that the 'consumer' does not know what they want.

The reason 'Step 1 - What is (or Empathy)' is absolutely critical is because the 'consumer' does not know exactly what they want. This is especially true of analytics. There is a great HBR article that talks about this. Often the 'consumer' knows what they don't want. Often the 'consumer' knows what they want only after they see the data visually expressed. How is he supposed to know that he is interested in 'Why sales have gone down in August', until he sees a dashboard that shows that sales have gone down in August. This is the concept of the 'Unknown Unknowns' when it comes to data analysis.

So if you are looking to apply Design Thinking principles to Dashboard Building, it should start with a collaborative conversation between the 'consumer' of the dashboard and the 'author' of the dashboard. Start with 'Step 1 - What is' and include the 'consumer' in this process. Understand what analysis is required, understand how they do that analysis today, understand what action is taken once that analysis is presented to the 'consumer'. Don't just build a dashboards with a set of KPIs they gave you. Don't assume they know why they want what they want. Sometimes asking the question - what action do you take once you consume the information in the dashboard, can lead to a very insightful conversation. How many of us receive reports that we never open? Are they even going to use this dashboard that I am creating?

In case you are wondering why Tableau is specifically suited to Design Thinking, the reason is that you can include the 'consumer' in every step of the design thinking process.

image from therightbraininitiative

While you can use a napkin to come up with ideas on how the dashboard can look, imagine using Tableau as a prototyping tool where you drag and drop data and ideate with the business user. While a napkin / whiteboard is great, when the 'consumer' sees their data visually come to life the engagement / involvement is off the chart. You will see them 'Lean In' and become part of the process. You've combined Steps 1-5 in one meeting.

As you use Tableau with the 'consumer' in the same room, you will know what wows and what doesn't. There is no guesswork and 'let me play around and get back to you in a couple days with options'. To do this you need a tool that is fast, a tool that keeps you in the 'flow' of analysis. A tool that takes the customer on a journey from ideas, to prototype to something that they can test, all in one collaborative meeting. In short, Tableau is Design Thinking on steroids when it comes to building dashboards.

Once you have got a working prototype in the meeting, you can go offline and 'Productionalize' what the business user wants.
